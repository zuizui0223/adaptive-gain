# Ecological novelty extension v1 — compositional novelty versus decision novelty

Status: post-freeze ecological extension to the machine-frozen V5 routeability manuscript. This document does not modify the frozen V5 submission surface.

## Central ecological inference

The target-relevant world reduction implies a distinction that is not yet explicit in the frozen V5 manuscript:

> **Compositional novelty is not necessarily decision novelty.**

Community composition can change while the decision-relevant structure of a focal interaction remains unchanged. Conversely, a small compositional change can create a new ecological branch and sharply alter effective complexity.

This follows from the same exact reduction already used to define decision-equivalence classes; no new theorem family is required.

## Exact structural basis

For a declared ecological target, represented states sharing:

1. the same focal target; and
2. the same remaining cross-target discrimination profile

belong to the same decision-equivalence class for the contingent-resolution problem.

The exact world-side quotient preserves the adaptive continuation value. Therefore, adding or replacing represented states inside an existing decision-equivalence class need not alter \(C_A\).

Ecological interpretation:

- taxonomic identity can change while the focal decision problem remains invariant;
- raw compositional turnover can therefore be **decision-silent**;
- a new state that creates a genuinely new branch-specific discrimination requirement can instead be **decision-novel**.

The second case can increase effective complexity even when species richness changes by only one.

## Connection to ecological novelty

Recent novel-ecosystem frameworks distinguish compositional novelty from functional and abiotic novelty and emphasize that species turnover alone does not determine system-level consequences (Kerr et al. 2026). In invasion ecology, novel species interactions are likewise understood as depending on how unfamiliar combinations alter interactions rather than on non-native identity alone (Saul & Jeschke 2015).

Decision novelty adds a different, task-specific dimension:

- **compositional novelty:** species identities or community composition differ from a reference state;
- **functional novelty:** traits or ecosystem functions differ;
- **decision novelty:** the set of distinctions that must be resolved for a focal ecological interaction changes.

These dimensions need not coincide.

## Ecological cases

### 1. Species invasion

An introduced resource or interaction partner can be compositionally novel but decision-silent if it falls into an existing decision-equivalence class for a focal consumer.

For example, an introduced host that elicits the same action and requires the same remaining discrimination sequence as an existing host increases taxonomic richness but need not increase contingent decision complexity.

By contrast, an introduced host that requires a new branch-specific cue can be decision-novel even if it is functionally similar in other ecological respects.

Claim ceiling:

> Decision novelty is not a predictor of invasion success or impact by itself. It predicts whether the introduction changes the information structure of a declared focal interaction.

### 2. Local extinction

Loss of one species from a decision-equivalence class can reduce taxonomic richness while leaving the focal decision structure unchanged as long as other equivalent states remain.

Loss of the only state representing a branch can instead collapse a decision class and reduce effective complexity disproportionately.

Thus biodiversity loss can be decision-silent or decision-structuring depending on where the lost state sits in the interaction-specific quotient.

### 3. Community turnover

Two communities can have high taxonomic beta diversity yet low decision novelty for a focal interaction if species replacements remain within corresponding decision-equivalence classes.

Conversely, modest compositional turnover can generate high decision novelty if replacements alter the branch structure of resource, host, predator or mutualist discrimination.

This suggests a task-specific distinction between:

\[
\text{taxonomic turnover}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{decision-structural turnover}.
\]

### 4. Climate-driven community change

Climate change can reorganize communities without necessarily changing effective decision complexity if replacements preserve the focal decision structure.

Alternatively, a small range shift introducing one interaction partner with a novel branch-specific requirement can change routeability without a large change in richness or conventional environmental distance.

This extends the V5 conclusion that environmental change matters when it changes ecological distinctions, not merely when it produces large compositional distance.

## Decision-structural turnover

The same quotient result implies a stronger statement about community turnover.

Taxonomic turnover and decision-structural turnover need not vary proportionally. A community can undergo extensive species replacement while preserving the same decision-equivalence classes for a focal interaction. In that case, taxonomic beta diversity can be high while the contingent decision problem remains invariant.

Conversely, a single replacement can introduce a new branch-specific discrimination requirement and alter routeability even when compositional distance is small.

This yields two qualitatively different kinds of turnover:

- **within-class turnover:** species identities change but replacements remain inside corresponding decision-equivalence classes; the focal contingent-resolution problem can remain unchanged;
- **branch-changing turnover:** at least one replacement creates, removes or reorganizes a decision-relevant branch; effective complexity can change disproportionately to taxonomic turnover.

Thus the relationship between compositional change and effective complexity can be **step-like rather than proportional**: large stretches of taxonomic turnover may be decision-silent, followed by a change in complexity when turnover crosses a decision-equivalence boundary.

This is analogous in spirit, but not equivalent, to the known decoupling between taxonomic and functional turnover. Empirical studies have shown that taxonomic homogenization need not be accompanied by functional homogenization, and can even coincide with functional differentiation when replacing species are functionally redundant or novel in different ways (White et al. 2018; Campbell & Mandrak 2020). Decision-structural turnover adds a third, focal-interaction-specific layer:

\[
\text{taxonomic turnover}
\;\not\equiv\;
\text{functional turnover}
\;\not\equiv\;
\text{decision-structural turnover}.
\]

The third quantity is not proposed as a universal beta-diversity metric. It asks only whether community change alters the distinctions that must be resolved for a declared ecological interaction.

### Consequence for biotic homogenization

Biotic homogenization is conventionally defined through declining compositional dissimilarity among assemblages, with taxonomic, functional and phylogenetic forms potentially changing in different directions. The routeability framework predicts another possible mismatch:

> Communities can become taxonomically more similar while remaining decision-distinct for a focal interaction, or become taxonomically different while remaining decision-equivalent.

Therefore, whether homogenization changes the information burden of an interaction depends on **which decision-equivalence classes are gained, lost or merged**, not simply on the magnitude or direction of taxonomic beta-diversity change.

This does not imply that decision structure determines ecosystem functioning or resilience. It identifies one mechanism by which the ecological consequences of compositional turnover can be weak, strong or threshold-like despite similar taxonomic change.

## Relation to functional redundancy

Decision equivalence should not be renamed functional redundancy.

Functional redundancy concerns similarity in ecosystem functions or trait-based roles among species. Decision equivalence is focal-interaction specific.

A pair of species can be:

- functionally redundant at the ecosystem level but decision-distinct for a predator or pollinator;
- functionally distinct yet decision-equivalent for a particular accept/reject action.

The exact quotient therefore provides an interaction-specific complement to functional redundancy rather than a replacement for it.

## Interaction-specific buffering under biodiversity loss

Decision-equivalence multiplicity creates a limited form of buffering against compositional loss.

If several species occupy the same decision-equivalence class for a focal interaction, losing one member need not change the contingent decision problem as long as at least one equivalent state remains represented. By contrast, loss of the last representative of that class can remove an entire decision branch.

This creates a threshold-like asymmetry:

- early losses within a multiply represented class can be decision-silent;
- loss of the final representative can be decision-structuring even if richness changes by only one.

The logic resembles ecological insurance and functional redundancy only at a very abstract level. Insurance theory concerns buffering of aggregate ecosystem properties through compensatory responses among species, while functional redundancy concerns overlap in ecological functions. The present result concerns invariance of one declared interaction-specific decision problem under replacement or loss.

Therefore we should not call this ecosystem resilience or biodiversity insurance. A safer ecological interpretation is:

> **Decision-equivalence can buffer the information structure of a focal interaction against some species turnover, but only until turnover crosses an equivalence-class boundary.**

This distinction is important because biodiversity–ecosystem-functioning research has repeatedly cautioned that apparent redundancy for one function or condition does not imply ecological dispensability, and response-diversity theory emphasizes that similarly functioning species can differ in their responses to environmental change (Mori et al. 2013; Biggs et al. 2020; Loreau et al. 2021; Eisenhauer et al. 2023).

## Candidate V5 insertion

A concise Results/Discussion addition could read:

> **Compositional turnover can be decision-silent or decision-novel.** The target-relevant reduction shows that ecological states belonging to the same decision-equivalence class can be exchanged or added without changing the optimal contingent resolution problem. Thus taxonomic turnover need not alter effective complexity. Conversely, a single new state can create a new branch-specific discrimination requirement and thereby change routeability disproportionately. This separates compositional novelty from decision novelty: what matters for the focal interaction is not simply whether community composition changes, but whether the change creates a new distinction that must be resolved.

## Why this extension is useful

It moves the routeability framework into three additional ecological domains without changing the mathematics:

1. invasion and novel species interactions;
2. biodiversity loss and redundancy;
3. community turnover under global change.

It also sharpens the V5 headline:

> **Diversity is not complexity, and compositional novelty is not necessarily decision novelty.**

## Claim firewall

- Do not claim that decision novelty predicts invasion success, ecological impact or ecosystem novelty overall.
- Do not equate decision equivalence with functional redundancy.
- Do not claim every introduced species increases effective complexity.
- Do not claim every within-class species loss is ecologically unimportant; the invariance is only for the declared focal decision problem.
- Do not call decision novelty a universal community metric. It remains task-specific and relational.
- No new theorem is introduced; all formal support comes from the existing target-relevant reduction.
