## Figure 1. Finite sensing architecture creates a dynamical no-go region

**A**, each ecological state `i` has its own adaptive-versus-fixed structural gap `g_i=C_F(i)-C_A(i)>=0`. Eco-evolutionary feedback is driven by the ordered between-state contrast `Delta g=g_2-g_1`, not by one state gap in isolation. **B**, the theorem does not require exact linearity between structural gap and selection. For any nondecreasing sensing-to-selection lift with marginal effect bounded by `L`, the state-to-state selection contrast satisfies `Delta s<=L Delta g`, and feedback is bounded by `G<=B L Delta g` for positive feedback-per-selection scale `B`. **C**, if even the maximum allowed feedback at the available structural contrast fails to exceed `G_osc`, the oscillatory regime is unreachable for every lift in the declared monotone-Lipschitz class. Crossing the bound means only that the regime is not ruled out; it does not guarantee oscillation.

Alt text: Three-panel conceptual diagram linking finite sensing to dynamical exclusion. Panel A shows a state-specific adaptive-versus-fixed sensing gap and the between-state contrast `Delta g`. Panel B shows admissible nonlinear sensing-to-selection curves bounded by marginal ceiling `L`, implying `Delta s<=L Delta g` and `G<=B L Delta g`. Panel C plots maximum feedback against available contrast, shading the region where the feedback ceiling does not exceed `G_osc` as unreachable and labeling the region beyond the necessary threshold as not ruled out but not guaranteed.

## Figure 2. Required feedback contrast forces a finite state-specific architecture

A requested local dynamical regime first imposes a required between-state structural contrast. Because every state gap is nonnegative, `Delta g>=q` implies that at least one ecological state must support `g_i>=q`; the sharp single-task finite bounds can then be applied to that high-gap state. **A**, for binary unit-cost sensing, required contrast `q=2` therefore forces at least one state capable of the exact first componentwise corner `(n,m,E)=(6,5,5)`, where `n` is represented alternatives, `m` cue resources and `E` irreducible fixed-side obligations. **B**, in the canonical nonlinear no-go example, binary architecture classes with `n<=5`, `m<=4`, or `E<=4` constrain every state to `0<=g_i<=1`; if both ecological states belong to such a class, their contrast satisfies `|Delta g|<=1` and cannot meet a requirement of two. **C**, with bounded arity greater than two the high-gap state requirement is generally Pareto-valued rather than one scalar minimum; for `q=3, b=4`, `(7,6,6)` and `(8,5,5)` are both nondominated.

Alt text: Flow diagram from requested oscillatory feedback to required structural contrast, then to a high-gap ecological state and finite sensing architecture. Panel A gives the binary `q=2` exact first corner `(6,5,5)`. Panel B crosses out the smaller binary classes `n<=5`, `m<=4`, and `E<=4`, each of which confines state gaps to at most one and therefore cannot supply contrast two. Panel C shows the bounded-arity Pareto trade-off for `q=3, b=4`, with nondominated points `(7,6,6)` and `(8,5,5)`.

## Figure 3. Ecology filters structurally generated selection through time

**A**, each recurrent ecological state carries both a finite sensing architecture, which generates state-indexed structural reward/selection, and a transition process that determines when the same reward recurs. **B**, slow ecological modes have large long-run evolutionary consequences only when the structurally generated reward vector aligns with those modes. **C**, for a reversible ecological transition process, `sigma_eff^2=sum_r w_r(1+r_r)/(1-r_r)`, separating temporal amplification by ecological persistence from reward-mode alignment `w_r`. Thus ecological persistence alone does not determine evolutionary timescale or cumulative effect.

Alt text: Three-panel schematic of ecological recurrence as a filter on structurally generated selection. Panel A links each community state both to its finite sensing architecture and state-specific selection contribution and to the transition operator controlling recurrence. Panel B contrasts a reward aligned with a slow ecological mode, which produces a large long-run contribution, with a weakly aligned reward, which produces a small contribution. Panel C gives the spectral expression for effective variance and emphasizes that slow-mode amplification matters only when reward-mode alignment is large.
