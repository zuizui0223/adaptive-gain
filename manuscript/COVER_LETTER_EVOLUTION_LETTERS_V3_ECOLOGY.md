Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Ecological structure and deadlines govern the evolutionary value of contingent sensing,”** for publication as a Letter in *Evolution Letters*.

Organisms often make ecological decisions before they can sample every potentially useful cue. Predator attacks, moving hosts, ephemeral resources and mating opportunities can impose time, energetic or exposure costs on information gathering, while later cues may matter only after earlier observations identify the relevant ecological context. We ask when this contingent organization of cue acquisition itself becomes selectively valuable.

Our central result is ecological rather than a generic information-theory claim. For a finite decision, contingent sensing has a unique performance advantage only under an intermediate ecological budget: constraints must be strong enough that a fixed, precommitted cue set cannot complete the decision, but weak enough that a routed cue sequence still can. This produces a non-monotonic prediction for the selective opportunity of contingent sensing across ecological constraint.

We further show that the relevant property of natural history is conditional cue dependence rather than simple cue abundance or marginal cue frequency. Large contingent advantages can occur even when every binary cue has equally frequent outcomes, because an early cue can determine which later cue is worth acquiring. This distinction leads to an empirical prediction: environmental heterogeneity should matter evolutionarily when it changes the target-relevant branch structure of decisions or moves organisms across the contingent-only budget window, not merely when environmental variance increases.

The theory then connects these individual decision episodes to eco-evolutionary dynamics. Ecological states that differ too little in contingent-sensing advantage cannot generate arbitrarily strong feedback through this mechanism, whereas recurring ecological states amplify sensing-generated selection only when the selection contrast aligns with persistent ecological modes.

The paper is motivated by the ecology of information, speed–accuracy trade-offs, resource specialization and proximate decision architecture. We use predator assessment, host/oviposition choice and pollinator flower choice as illustrative natural-history mappings and provide an explicit empirical workflow for measuring cue sequences, stopping decisions and ecological budgets. Exact finite proofs and structural bounds are retained in the Supplement so that the main Letter remains centered on ecological predictions and empirical interpretation.

We believe the manuscript fits *Evolution Letters* because it links a proximate feature of information use—conditional cue acquisition—to testable predictions about selection in variable environments and to limits on eco-evolutionary dynamics. The theory predicts not simply that information is valuable, but when natural history exposes the value of acquiring the right information next.
