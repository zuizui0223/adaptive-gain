# Ecological framing v1 — contingent sensing under natural-history constraints

Status: ecological narrative map for a post-freeze V4 candidate. The V3 mathematical freeze remains unchanged.

## Central ecological question

The paper should no longer open with finite decision architecture as a mathematical object. It should open with an ecological observation:

> Organisms often have to act before they can sample every potentially useful cue.

Predator attack, moving prey or hosts, ephemeral flowers, mate encounters, oviposition opportunities, and exposure to natural enemies make information gathering time-limited. Empirical and conceptual work in behavioral ecology already treats information acquisition as costly, and speed–accuracy trade-offs are documented across ecologically relevant decisions. The unresolved question for this paper is more specific:

> **When does contingent cue acquisition itself become selectively valuable, and what ecological structure creates that value?**

The answer supplied by the repository is not “when environments are more uncertain” or “when cues are more informative” in a marginal sense. It is when three ecological ingredients coincide:

1. a decision can be decomposed into branch-specific cue requirements;
2. a hard or effectively hard ecological budget lies between adaptive and fixed resolution costs; and/or
3. ecological states differ enough in this branch structure for selection generated by contingent sensing to alter feedback dynamics.

This reframes the paper from a theorem about finite sensing into a theory of **ecological exposure of contingent sensing**.

## Literature-supported background

### Information is useful only through decisions

Dall et al. (2005) explicitly framed animal information use in terms of ecological uncertainty, costs of information gathering, and statistical decision theory. Schmidt et al. (2010) broadened this into an “ecology of information,” emphasizing consequences of information use from individual decisions to populations and communities.

**Narrative use:** cite these papers to establish that information has ecological value only insofar as it changes action, and that the ecological consequences of information use are the broader target.

### Information gathering consumes time and can expose organisms to risk

Chittka, Skorupski & Raine (2009) reviewed speed–accuracy trade-offs in prey/predator detection, pollinator flower choice and spatial exploration, emphasizing that higher-quality information often requires longer sampling. Lima & Dill (1990) showed that predation risk shapes feeding, escape and other behavioral decisions, providing a general ecological reason why “decision time” can itself carry fitness consequences.

**Narrative use:** this supports interpreting the budget B as time, exposure or opportunity loss rather than as an abstract computational budget.

### Natural-history complexity can make discrimination costly

Bernays & Wcislo (1994) argued that informational complexity in food/host choice carries costs in decision time, efficiency and mortality exposure, and that sensory focusing can reduce these costs. Bernays (2001) reviewed evidence that generalist herbivores can be less efficient in host choice/discrimination than specialists when processing multiple sensory inputs.

**Narrative use:** this gives an ecological bridge to branch-specific cue repertoires and specialization/generalism. The paper should not claim that the theorem explains specialization, but it can predict when contingent routing can mitigate the burden of a broad cue repertoire.

### Proximate sensing architecture matters evolutionarily

Eliassen et al. (2016) argued that evolutionary models should explicitly represent sensing, information processing and behavioral control, rather than only optimized actions.

**Narrative use:** this places the paper in a proximate-to-evolution tradition. Our contribution is an exact finite limit on which contingent architectures can matter under ecological constraints.

## Ecological headline

Recommended headline:

> **Ecological structure and deadlines govern the evolutionary value of contingent sensing.**

The mathematical no-go theorem remains important, but it should become one consequence of the ecological argument rather than the opening claim.

## Main biological predictions

### Prediction 1 — Contingent sensing is favored most strongly at intermediate constraint

If the ecological budget is too small, neither contingent nor fixed sensing resolves the decision. If the budget is very relaxed, both do. The unique performance advantage of contingent sensing exists only when the budget lies between the two resolution costs.

Ecological interpretation:

- very severe time/exposure limitation -> information use collapses because even the adaptive sequence is too slow;
- intermediate limitation -> contingent sampling can succeed while exhaustive/precommitted sampling cannot;
- weak limitation -> the performance advantage disappears because all relevant cues can be sampled.

This is a **non-monotonic prediction** with respect to ecological constraint.

### Prediction 2 — Branch exclusivity matters more than marginal cue prevalence

A cue can be globally common, balanced or individually weak yet still be valuable because it determines which later cue becomes relevant. Conversely, many available cues need not create a large adaptive advantage if the same cues are needed in every branch.

Ecological interpretation:

> selection should track conditional cue dependence — which cues become useful after which observations — rather than raw cue number, cue entropy or environmental variance alone.

### Prediction 3 — Environmental heterogeneity matters only when it changes the decision problem

Two habitats, seasons or community states can differ strongly in raw environmental variables but produce the same target-relevant sensing structure. Such differences should not generate selection on contingent architecture in this model.

Conversely, modest ecological changes can matter strongly if they alter:

- which alternatives require discrimination;
- which branch-specific cues are needed;
- whether the organism crosses the adaptive-only budget window.

Thus “environmental variability” should be replaced by **variability in decision architecture** as the relevant predictor.

### Prediction 4 — Persistent environments matter only when selection aligns with the persistent mode

Slowly changing ecological states do not automatically generate long evolutionary memory. Persistence matters when the states that recur slowly are also the states carrying strong differences in contingent-sensing payoff.

This predicts an interaction:

> ecological persistence × state-specific sensing advantage,

not a main effect of persistence alone.

### Prediction 5 — Generalism can favor routing rather than exhaustive sensing

A generalist facing many hosts, prey, flowers or microhabitats may not need to process every diagnostic cue on every encounter. A coarse early cue can route the organism to a smaller branch-specific repertoire.

This suggests a conditional prediction, not a universal explanation of specialization:

- when branch structure is strong and early routing cues are cheap, contingent sensing can reduce the decision burden of generalism;
- when many terminal cues are required across all branches, fixed sensory/processing burden grows and specialization or sensory focusing may remain favored.

## Natural-history mappings for the paper

These are conceptual mappings, not empirical demonstrations.

### Predator assessment

Worlds: predator types, distances or attack contexts that require different responses.

Early cue: broad risk class or direction.

Terminal cue: identity-, distance- or trajectory-specific information needed only within some branches.

Budget: time before attack or safe escape opportunity closes.

### Host / oviposition choice

Worlds: candidate host categories or plant states.

Early cue: habitat/plant identity.

Terminal cue: chemical, surface or quality cue relevant only within a subset.

Budget: host movement, handling time, exposure to enemies, or oviposition opportunity.

This system is especially well motivated by Bernays & Wcislo (1994) and Bernays (2001).

### Pollinator flower choice

Worlds: rewarding/non-rewarding flower classes or flower species.

Early cue: coarse visual or spatial cue.

Terminal cue: fine color, odor or handling cue used only for a subset.

Budget: foraging time and opportunity cost.

Speed–accuracy trade-offs in bees provide a concrete empirical precedent for time-sensitive discrimination, without implying that the present finite model has already been fitted to bees.

## Discussion architecture

The Discussion should answer five ecological questions in order:

1. **When should contingent sensing be selected?**  
   Under intermediate deadlines/resource ceilings, not monotonically with “difficulty.”

2. **What property of natural history creates the advantage?**  
   Branch-specific cue requirements and conditional relevance, not cue abundance or marginal balance.

3. **How does environmental change create fluctuating selection?**  
   By moving ecological states across decision architectures or across the adaptive-only budget interval.

4. **When does that selection matter for eco-evolutionary dynamics?**  
   Only when structural contrasts are large enough and when recurring ecological modes align with the reward/selection contrast.

5. **How can this be tested?**  
   Measure cue sequences and stopping rules, manipulate deadlines, reconstruct target-relevant decision trees, compare observed contingent strategies with fixed-counterfactual cue sets, and test whether fitness differences peak in the predicted intermediate window.

## Claim firewall

- Do not equate C_A/C_F with neural processing time unless a focal empirical system justifies that mapping.
- Do not claim branch-specific sensing explains ecological specialization in general.
- Do not claim environmental variability alone selects for contingent sensing.
- Do not claim crossing the structural no-go threshold guarantees oscillation.
- Do not call the model a general theory of Shannon information.
- Keep all natural-history examples explicitly illustrative until fitted to data.
