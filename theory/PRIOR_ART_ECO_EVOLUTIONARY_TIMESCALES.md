# Prior-art boundary: eco-evolutionary timescales and fluctuating selection

## Purpose

This note prevents the evolutionary-timescale branch from claiming novelty for established ideas.

The branch does **not** claim as new:

- eco-evolutionary feedbacks;
- rapid or contemporary evolution;
- fluctuating selection;
- temporal autocorrelation of selection;
- temporal reversals in directional selection;
- plasticity, genetic tracking, or bet-hedging in autocorrelated environments;
- the possibility that rapid short-term changes coexist with long-term stasis;
- individual behavior or information use affecting ecological interactions and community dynamics;
- the standard haploid log-odds selection recurrence;
- partial-sum autocovariance identities, AR(1) scaling, or two-state Markov moments;
- apparent negative evolutionary rate-time scaling;
- the macroevolutionary observation that substantial evolutionary activity need not accumulate into large net change.

The proposed contribution is narrower: use the repository's exact finite sensing structures to specify an **upstream mechanistic source of state-dependent selection**, then separate the amount of instantaneous structural opportunity from the temporal coherence and directional bias that determine long-run retention.

---

## 1. Fluctuating selection and rapid-but-bounded evolution are established

Bell (2010) explicitly argued that natural selection can be strong while fluctuating in intensity and direction, and that repeated environmental and selective change can reconcile rapid adaptation with limited long-term directional change.

**Reference**

Bell, G. (2010). Fluctuating selection: the perpetual renewal of adaptation in variable environments. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 365(1537), 87–97. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0150

Messer, Ellner & Hairston (2016) emphasized that heritable phenotypes can change markedly in only a few generations while longer-term trajectories remain bounded when selection fluctuates in direction.

**Reference**

Messer, P. W., Ellner, S. P., & Hairston, N. G. Jr. (2016). Can Population Genetics Adapt to Rapid Evolution? *Trends in Genetics*, 32(7), 408–418. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tig.2016.04.005

Therefore the statement

```text
strong short-term selection + reversals -> little long-term net change
```

is prior art.

---

## 2. Temporal autocorrelation of selection is established theory

Chevin & Haller (2014) analyzed the temporal variance and autocorrelation of directional selection gradients under fixed and moving optima. A key warning is directly relevant here: very different underlying processes can generate similar temporal distributions of estimated selection gradients, so variance and autocorrelation of selection alone need not identify the mechanism that produced them.

**Reference**

Chevin, L.-M., & Haller, B. C. (2014). The temporal distribution of directional gradients under selection for an optimum. *Evolution*, 68(12), 3381–3394. https://doi.org/10.1111/evo.12532

Tufto (2015) developed a quantitative-genetic model in which genetic tracking, plasticity, and bet-hedging respond jointly to temporally autocorrelated fluctuating selection. Slow fluctuations can favour genetic tracking and plasticity; fast fluctuations limit those tracking responses.

**Reference**

Tufto, J. (2015). Genetic evolution, plasticity, and bet-hedging as adaptive responses to temporally autocorrelated fluctuating selection: A quantitative genetic model. *Evolution*, 69(8), 2034–2049. https://doi.org/10.1111/evo.12716

Accordingly, the present branch does **not** claim novelty for the statements

```text
temporal autocorrelation matters for adaptation
```

or

```text
positive versus negative/weak temporal coherence changes long-run response.
```

The proposed distinction is upstream: instead of taking the selection-gradient process as primitive, the repository asks whether changes in community-dependent cue obligations can generate different selection states through already-solved continuation and productive-frontier structures.

This is especially important in light of Chevin & Haller's mechanism-identification warning. The structural layer is intended as a candidate mechanistic state description, not as a claim that selection-gradient time series alone reveal that structure empirically.

---

## 3. Eco-evolutionary feedbacks are established

Post & Palkovacs (2009) defined eco-evolutionary feedbacks as reciprocal interactions in which ecological change alters evolution and evolved organismal differences feed back to ecological interactions, communities, or ecosystems.

**Reference**

Post, D. M., & Palkovacs, E. P. (2009). Eco-evolutionary feedbacks in community and ecosystem ecology: interactions between the ecological theatre and the evolutionary play. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 364, 1629–1640. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0012

Govaert et al. (2019) reviewed theoretical eco-evolutionary feedback models across populations, communities, abiotic environments, and spatial scales.

**Reference**

Govaert, L., Fronhofer, E. A., Lion, S., et al. (2019). Eco-evolutionary feedbacks—Theoretical models and perspectives. *Functional Ecology*, 33(1), 13–30. https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2435.13241

Therefore the generic loop

```text
ecology -> evolution -> ecology
```

is not a repository novelty.

---

## 4. Individual behavior and information can scale up to communities

The step from individual phenotype or behavior to altered ecological interactions is also established.

Werner & Peacor (2003) reviewed trait-mediated indirect interactions in ecological communities: phenotypic or behavioral responses can alter per-capita interaction effects and thereby change population or community outcomes.

**Reference**

Werner, E. E., & Peacor, S. D. (2003). A review of trait-mediated indirect interactions in ecological communities. *Ecology*, 84(5), 1083–1100. https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084[1083:AROTII]2.0.CO;2

Gil et al. (2018) synthesized how social information use can connect individual behavior to population and community dynamics, including competition, species interactions, density dependence, and extinction risk.

**Reference**

Gil, M. A., Hein, A. M., Spiegel, O., Baskett, M. L., & Sih, A. (2018). Social Information Links Individual Behavior to Population and Community Dynamics. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*, 33(7), 535–548. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2018.04.010

Therefore the generic statement

```text
individual information/behavior -> interaction changes -> community consequences
```

is prior art.

The present branch makes a narrower structural claim: the exact continuation/frontier mathematics can distinguish **which higher-order cue configurations change the advantage of contingent sensing**, thereby supplying one explicit state-dependent selection mechanism inside that established individual-to-community framework.

## 5. Micro–macro timescaling and evolutionary stasis are established problems

Harmon et al. (2021) reviewed apparent timescaling across molecular, phenotypic, and diversification rates. Estimated rates are typically highest over the shortest intervals, creating an apparent disconnect between the large amount of short-term evolutionary potential and the much smaller amount expressed over macroevolutionary time.

**Reference**

Harmon, L. J., Pennell, M. W., Henao-Diaz, L. F., Rolland, J., Sipley, B. N., & Uyeda, J. C. (2021). Causes and Consequences of Apparent Timescaling Across All Estimated Evolutionary Rates. *Annual Review of Ecology, Evolution, and Systematics*, 52, 587–609. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-011921-023644

Voje (2016), using 450 fossil time series, showed that stasis need not mean absence of evolutionary movement: nonaccumulating morphological fluctuations during stasis can traverse substantial distances in morphospace even when net long-term change remains small.

**Reference**

Voje, K. L. (2016). Tempo does not correlate with mode in the fossil record. *Evolution*, 70(12), 2678–2689. https://doi.org/10.1111/evo.13090

The fossil literature more generally treats sustained directional transformations as uncommon over geological time and long-term phenotypic stasis as a real pattern requiring process-based explanation.

Therefore the conceptual contrast

```text
large total evolutionary activity != large net long-term displacement
```

is not new.

The repository-specific move is to give the short-term activity an explicit **structural opportunity generator** upstream of selection and to keep the later retention process mathematically separate.

## 6. Rate-time scaling should not be the main observable

De Lisle & Svensson (2026) revisited evolutionary rate–time relationships and showed that much negative rate-time scaling follows from the mathematical dependence created by dividing evolutionary change by elapsed time. Their reanalysis found that over 99% of variation in rate-time relationships across six datasets was explained by variation in time. They recommend focusing more directly on how accumulated evolutionary change varies with time.

**Reference**

De Lisle, S. P., & Svensson, E. I. (2026). Revisiting evolutionary rate–time relationships. *Evolution*, 80(1), 28–39. https://doi.org/10.1093/evolut/qpaf222

The branch therefore does **not** use

\[
\text{evolutionary rate}=\frac{\text{change}}{\text{time}}
\]

as its central cross-timescale observable.

Instead it separates

- instantaneous structural opportunity `Omega`;
- total absolute selection/evolutionary activity;
- signed cumulative change;
- retained displacement and retained fraction;
- temporal coherence `phi`;
- long-run directional bias `m`;
- directional trend-emergence horizon `H_x`.

This aligns the theory with accumulated change and process structure rather than denominator-confounded rate-time plots.

## 7. What the repository adds to this literature

The proposed bridge starts one mechanistic layer earlier than standard fluctuating-selection models.

A community state `X` is mapped to a finite sensing task

\[
\mathcal T(X).
\]

Existing repository theorems then give

\[
C_A(X)=V_A(\operatorname{Type}(X))
\]

and

\[
C_F(X)=\tau_c(\mathcal H_{\min}(X)).
\]

Before selecting a fitness map, define the structural opportunity

\[
\boxed{
\Omega(X)=\frac{C_F(X)-C_A(X)}{C_A(X)}.
}
\]

The already-proved sharp bounded-arity theorem immediately yields

\[
\boxed{
\Omega_b^*(n,m)=R_b(n,m)-1.
}
\]

The productive-frontier edge-cap and rank-cap theorems then yield a nontrivial ecological contrast:

```text
number of irreducible productive obligations
    -> can cap worst-case opportunity

maximum size of one obligation
    -> cannot cap worst-case opportunity for any positive rank cap
```

because rank-one singleton obligations already attain the extremum.

Under the deliberately minimal continuous fitness lift,

\[
\boxed{
s(X)=\lambda[C_F(X)-C_A(X)]-\kappa.}
\]

The exact existing decomposition

\[
C_F-C_A=(U-C_A)-(C_U-C_F)-(U-C_U)
\]

separates state-specific structural selection into

```text
branch-exclusive opportunity
- external shortcut discount
- internal union redundancy.
```

Across community states,

\[
\boxed{
\Delta s
=\lambda\Delta C_F
-\lambda\Delta C_A
-\Delta\kappa,
}
\]

separating productive-frontier rewiring from adaptive-continuation rewiring.

The nonlinear hard-budget lift uses the original theorem even more directly: contingent sensing alone can succeed exactly when

\[
C_A(X)\le B<C_F(X).
\]

The registered `resource_role_profile_collision()` sharpens the mechanism claim: two tasks can have the same adaptive continuation root type and the same multiset of complete per-resource role profiles while `C_F` differs. Under either the continuous or hard-budget lift, this higher-order co-location difference can reverse selection.

This is the proposed repository-specific modeling contribution.

## 8. What time adds after structural selection is generated

Once a biological map produces state-dependent selection `s_t`, the temporal layer is deliberately conventional but useful.

For zero-mean fixed-magnitude selection with geometric correlation `phi`,

\[
A_H=O(H),
\qquad
R_H^{\rm RMS}=O(\sqrt H),
\]

and

\[
\mathcal R_H^{\rm RMS}
\sim
\sqrt{\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}}H^{-1/2}.
\]

If the selection sign has nonzero stationary mean `m`, then

\[
E[S_H]=\delta mH
\]

and the RMS retained fraction approaches `|m|` for finite correlation time.

A weak long-run bias can nevertheless remain hidden beneath correlated short-term fluctuations for an approximate horizon

\[
\boxed{
H_\times
\approx
\frac{1-m^2}{m^2}
\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}.
}
\]

Thus the theory distinguishes

```text
selection magnitude
!= temporal coherence
!= long-run directional bias
!= observation horizon.
```

These formulas are not claimed as new stochastic-process theory. Their purpose is to keep the temporal filter explicit once the upstream structural mechanism has generated selection.

## 9. Cross-scale result specific to the combined construction

The temporal-routing branch and evolutionary-timescale branch use temporal predictability differently.

In the symmetric within-individual routing model,

\[
G_{route}\propto|\phi|.
\]

Both persistence (`phi>0`) and predictable alternation (`phi<0`) can make the next useful cue predictable.

By contrast, long-term directional evolutionary retention depends on signed temporal coherence and long-run directional bias. Perfect alternation can therefore maximize within-individual predictability while cancelling long-horizon directional displacement exactly over even windows.

The combined statement is

\[
\boxed{
\text{predictability useful for individual contingent behavior}
\ne
\text{temporal coherence/bias required for directional evolutionary accumulation}.
}
\]

This cross-scale sign asymmetry is a repository-specific synthesis, not a claim that the component temporal formulas are individually novel.

## 10. What still must be shown before a strong biological claim

The branch remains theoretical until at least one natural system supplies evidence for the full chain

```text
community state
-> feasible cue graph / productive obligations
-> different contingent-vs-fixed structural opportunity
-> measurable fitness difference among sensory architectures
-> altered interaction edges
-> feedback to community state
-> temporally varying selection
-> long-run occupancy/coherence structure.
```

Without those links, the repository supports structural possibility theorems, sharp constraints, and evolutionary hypotheses—not a demonstrated natural eco-evolutionary mechanism.
