# When does a long-term evolutionary trend emerge from rapid short-term fluctuation?

## Status

This note derives a practical crossover timescale from the standard moment model
used in `DIRECTIONAL_BIAS_AND_LONG_TERM_ACCUMULATION.md`. The probability algebra
is not claimed as new. Its purpose is to make the short-term/long-term distinction
quantitative in the structural eco-evolutionary framework.

---

## 1. Mean trend versus fluctuation scale

Let

\[
s_t=\delta X_t,
\qquad X_t\in\{-1,+1\},
\]

with

\[
E[X_t]=m
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+k})=(1-m^2)\phi^k.
\]

For cumulative selection

\[
S_H=\sum_{t=1}^Hs_t,
\]

we have

\[
E[S_H]=\delta mH
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Var}(S_H)
=\delta^2(1-m^2)F_H(\phi).
\]

Define a directional signal-to-noise ratio

\[
\boxed{
D_H
=
\frac{|E[S_H]|}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_H)}}
=
\frac{|m|H}{\sqrt{(1-m^2)F_H(\phi)}}.
}
\]

The selection magnitude `delta` cancels.

Interpretation:

```text
D_H << 1 : cumulative trajectory is fluctuation-dominated
D_H ~ 1  : directional trend becomes comparable to fluctuation scale
D_H >> 1 : persistent directional component dominates
```

---

## 2. Long-horizon approximation

For fixed

\[
-1<\phi<1,
\]

\[
F_H(\phi)
\sim
H\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}.
\]

Therefore

\[
D_H
\sim
\frac{|m|}{\sqrt{1-m^2}}
\sqrt{
H\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}
}.
\]

The directional component eventually dominates whenever

\[
m\ne0.
\]

But the horizon required can be very long when the bias is weak.

---

## 3. Directional trend emergence time

Define the asymptotic crossover by

\[
D_H\approx1.
\]

Solving gives

\[
\boxed{
H_\times
\approx
\frac{1-m^2}{m^2}
\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}.
}
\]

This is the minimal **directional trend emergence timescale**.

It answers a different question from generation time or environmental correlation
time:

> how long must one observe a system before a persistent but noisy directional
> evolutionary bias becomes larger than the fluctuation scale generated by
> reversible short-term evolution?

---

## 4. Small bias creates a very long hidden-trend regime

For

\[
|m|\ll1,
\]

\[
H_\times
\approx
\frac{1}{m^2}
\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}.
\]

Thus the crossover diverges quadratically as directional bias approaches zero.

Halving a small bias roughly quadruples the observation horizon needed for the
long-term trend to emerge from fluctuations.

Example at temporal independence:

\[
m=0.1,\quad\phi=0
\]

gives

\[
H_\times\approx99.
\]

Strong evolutionary movement can therefore be visible over many short studies
while the weak persistent direction remains statistically or visually obscured
for roughly a century of generations.

---

## 5. Positive temporal coherence can delay trend detection

The coherence factor is

\[
\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}.
\]

For fixed `m`, positive `phi` increases the crossover horizon.

For example, with

\[
m=0.1,
\]

we obtain approximately

```text
phi = -0.5  -> H_x ~ 33
phi =  0    -> H_x ~ 99
phi = +0.5  -> H_x ~ 297
```

This may seem counterintuitive. Positive autocorrelation makes selection direction
more persistent over short runs, which can make contemporary evolution look very
strong. But those same long runs inflate the correlated fluctuation variance
around a weak long-term mean, so more generations are needed before the small
persistent directional bias dominates.

Negative temporal correlation does the opposite: rapid reversals cancel
fluctuation variance more efficiently, allowing a small occupancy imbalance to
emerge sooner as a long-term trend.

---

## 6. Connection to community-state occupancy

The directional bias `m` need not be an intrinsic property of the organism.
It can arise from how often the eco-evolutionary system occupies community states
that favour opposite sensory architectures.

Suppose two community states produce selection coefficients

\[
+s
\]

and

\[
-s.
\]

If their stationary occupancies are

\[
\pi_+
\quad\text{and}\quad
\pi_-=1-\pi_+,
\]

then

\[
\boxed{
m=\pi_+-\pi_-=2\pi_+-1.}
\]

A perfectly balanced community-state process has

\[
m=0
\]

and no linear long-term trend.

A small occupancy imbalance gives

\[
|m|>0,
\]

but the resulting trend can remain hidden beneath reversible short-term evolution
for a horizon of order

\[
1/m^2.
\]

Thus long-term evolutionary time can be controlled by **community occupancy
imbalance**, not only by selection intensity within any one state.

## 7. Connection to the repository's higher-order structural collision

The registered collision provides two community-like sensing states with

\[
(C_A,C_F)=(2,2)
\]

and

\[
(C_A,C_F)=(2,3),
\]

while retaining the same adaptive continuation root type and the same multiset of
complete per-resource role profiles.

At a suitable shared maintenance cost, these states produce equal-and-opposite
selection.

The current note adds a new timescale interpretation:

- the structural collision determines **which state favours which sensory
  architecture**;
- the temporal transition process determines `phi`;
- the long-run occupancy imbalance determines `m`;
- together, `m` and `phi` determine how many generations are needed before a
  directional evolutionary trend emerges from the short-term oscillations.

So higher-order community cue structure can generate the sign of selection, while
community-state occupancy and persistence determine the timescale on which that
selection becomes visible as a long-term trend.

## 8. Four regimes

The combined theory distinguishes at least four limiting regimes.

### High opportunity, m=0, phi near -1

Strong short-term reversals with very rapid cancellation.

### High opportunity, m=0, phi positive

Strong persistent-looking short-term runs, but no asymptotic directional trend;
retained fraction still vanishes.

### High opportunity, small nonzero m

Rapid short-term evolution plus a weak long-term trend that may require many
generations to emerge.

### Nonzero m with large horizon H >> H_x

Directional accumulation dominates; the system now looks macro-temporally
trending rather than fluctuation-dominated.

The distinction is therefore not simply

```text
fast evolution versus slow evolution
```

but

```text
large instantaneous activity
x temporal coherence
x long-run directional imbalance
x observation horizon.
```

---

## 9. Scope boundary

The crossover is an asymptotic signal-to-noise timescale in a stationary
fixed-magnitude sign model. It is not a universal estimator of evolutionary trend
onset.

It does not yet include

- changing selection magnitudes;
- nonstationary climate or geological trends;
- demographic stochasticity;
- drift, mutation, migration, or diploidy;
- state transition probabilities altered by evolving allele frequency;
- quantitative-trait genetic covariance changes;
- speciation or lineage turnover.

Its role is to isolate a clean theoretical reason why a system can show rapid
contemporary evolution for many generations before a weak but persistent
long-term directional trend becomes visible.
